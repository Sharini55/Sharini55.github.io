FSE 100 AR/VR Educational Platform

View Transition Doc See Prototype Demo

Problem Statement

Following a corporate donation of 30+ Meta Quest VR headsets by Honeywell to ASU's Fulton Schools of Engineering (FSE), the initial scope tasked our team with building an isolated 2-to-3-day VR curriculum module specifically for freshmen in the introductory FSE 100 course[cite: 2].

However, early stakeholder discovery with university faculty revealed a fundamental flaw in the initial requirement: locking immersive technology strictly into a single freshmen course created massive operational constraints, high scheduling friction, and a single point of failure. Without a scalable model, the donated hardware risked becoming obsolete after a single semester.

Target Users

Undergraduate Engineering Students requiring hands-on, spatial understanding of complex engineering concepts (such as robotics debugging, circuit visualization, and spatial mechanics)[cite: 2].

Course Instructors & TAs who need a modular, low-friction tool that integrates smoothly into existing curricula without requiring complex software setups or rigid, multi-hour scheduling slots[cite: 2, 3].

Strategic Pivot & Platform Solution

Instead of building a hyper-specific, one-off VR lesson plan, our team executed a strategic product pivot[cite: 2]. We redefined the product vision from a rigid single-course module to an extensible, multi-course academic platform framework[cite: 2].

The core value delivery: We designed modular, high-yield activity blocks (e.g., an interactive EV3 Lego Robot Maze Runner for logic debugging)[cite: 2] paired with lightweight WebXR/Three.js web previews and Unity concept builds[cite: 2, 3]. To ensure long-term sustainability, we delivered a comprehensive transition design document that acts as an operational roadmap for future engineering development cohorts to scale the ecosystem[cite: 2].

Technical & PM Toolkit

The project combined rapid prototyping technologies with structured product discovery and decision matrices to evaluate implementation trade-offs across hardware, engine overhead, and user friction[cite: 2].

User Research & Discovery

We conducted quantitative discovery surveys and qualitative interviews ($N=10$) with active engineering undergraduates to evaluate emerging technology literacy and surface friction points with existing campus VR installations (such as ASU Dreamscape)[cite: 2, 3].

Key Finding: 70% of surveyed students had completed a Dreamscape VR session, but sentiment was strongly split[cite: 2]. Students cited motion sickness, hardware glitches, and forced, rigid narrative storylines as major turn-offs[cite: 2]. They explicitly demanded high-interactivity, practical hands-on debugging over passive story-driven experiences[cite: 2].

Product Deliverables

1 — Scalable Transition Design Document

Authored a comprehensive operational and technical design doc outlining user personas, technical dependencies, hardware management guidelines, and roadmap expansion plans to seamlessly hand off development to future engineering cohorts[cite: 2].

2 — Weighted Decision Matrix for Product Direction

Evaluated 6 distinct concept directions (Dreamscape builds, custom modules, tech lectures, interview prep, activity-based tools) using a weighted matrix prioritized on Student Engagement (50%) and Implementation Feasibility (20%)[cite: 2].

3 — Interactive Web & VR Prototypes

Spearheaded early concept validation by deploying a Three.js WebXR concept on Vercel and a Unity-based EV3 Lego Robot Maze Runner simulation to gather real-time feedback from course professors[cite: 2, 3].

What I Learned and APM Takeaways

1. Pivot early based on real constraints: The biggest risk in emerging tech projects is building a polished solution for the wrong scope. By challenging the initial "single course" requirement through user discovery, we saved months of engineering overhead and built a framework that scales across the university[cite: 2].

2. Engagement is killed by unnecessary friction: VR is intrinsically immersive, but poor user experience (forced narratives, hardware fatigue) rapidly degrades value. High-yield, short-burst interactive tasks perform significantly better in academic settings[cite: 2].
